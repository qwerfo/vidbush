# Hoover Dam: Taming the Colorado

## The River

In the summer of 1931, the bottom of Black Canyon was one of the hottest places in America. On some days the thermometer reached 120 degrees, and the rock walls held the heat long after dark.

Through that canyon ran the Colorado River. Each spring it flooded the farms downstream. Each summer it shrank to a trickle. The plan was simple to say and almost impossible to do: stop the river cold, and put it to work.

## The Men

The country was sinking into the Great Depression. When word spread that men were needed on the Colorado, the jobless came by the thousands, many with their families in tow.

They camped in tents and cardboard shacks on the bare desert, in a place people called Ragtown. To house the workers, the government built a new town called Boulder City, about 30 miles from a small railroad stop named Las Vegas.

The contract went to Six Companies, a group of western builders who joined forces for the job. Their superintendent was Frank Crowe, a blunt, tireless engineer who had spent his life building dams in the West.

## Moving a River

Before anyone could pour a dam, the river had to go somewhere else. Crews blasted four giant tunnels through the canyon walls, each one about 56 feet across.

In November 1932, the Colorado was turned into the tunnels, and the riverbed went dry for the first time in human memory. Then the workers dug down through the mud and gravel until they hit solid rock. Upstream and downstream, earth and rock barriers called cofferdams kept the river from creeping back.

## The High Scalers

The canyon walls were loose and dangerous, so someone had to clean them. That job belonged to the high scalers.

They swung from ropes hundreds of feet above the river, armed with jackhammers, crowbars and dynamite. They pried away loose rock so it could not fall on the men below. Some became local legends for their daring. Crowds gathered on the rim just to watch them work.

The work was brutal. Heat, falling rock and blasting accidents all took their toll. The number most often cited is 112 deaths connected with building the dam.

## Concrete Without End

Then came the concrete. If the dam had been poured as one solid mass, the heat of the curing cement would have taken many decades to escape, and the dam would have cracked.

So the engineers built it in interlocking blocks, like giant bricks, and ran pipes through them carrying ice cold water. Buckets the size of small rooms swung out over the canyon on cables, day and night.

By the time they were done, they had placed about 3.25 million cubic yards of concrete in the dam itself. That is enough to pave a highway from San Francisco to New York.

## Ahead of Schedule

Under Crowe's relentless pace, the work raced forward. Over the life of the project, about 21,000 people worked on the dam.

President Franklin Roosevelt dedicated it in September 1935. The job was finished in 1936, roughly two years ahead of schedule.

The dam rose 726 feet from bedrock, taller than a sixty story building. Behind it, the water slowly gathered into Lake Mead, which became the largest reservoir in the United States by capacity.

## Power for the West

During the Second World War, its power helped drive the factories of Southern California. Today, the turbines at Hoover Dam still turn, sending electricity to homes in Nevada, Arizona and California.

The river that once flooded and starved the land now waters cities and farms across the Southwest.

It was built by men who had nowhere else to go, in a canyon that seemed made to defeat them. Nearly a century later, it still stands.
