# The Erie Canal: The Ditch That Built New York

On the morning of July 4, 1817, near the village of Rome, New York, a small crowd gathered to watch a man push a spade into the earth. It did not look like much. But that first shovel of dirt began one of the boldest projects in American history.

The plan was to dig a canal across the state, from Albany on the Hudson River to Buffalo on the shore of Lake Erie. The route ran 363 miles through forest, swamp and solid rock. Few Americans had any formal training as engineers, and none had ever built anything like it.

The man behind the idea was DeWitt Clinton, the governor of New York. He believed a water highway to the Great Lakes would make his state the gateway to the West. His critics were not so sure. They mocked the project as Clinton's Ditch, and some called it Clinton's Folly. Thomas Jefferson is said to have called a canal of that size little short of madness.

Clinton pushed ahead anyway, and the state paid for the work itself. The canal would cost about seven million dollars, an enormous sum in those days. Many lawmakers feared it would bankrupt the state before a single boat ever floated on it.

The crews were local farmers, hired hands and, later, many Irish immigrants. They worked with shovels, picks, axes and horses. They invented new tools as they went, including a stump puller that could rip a tree out by its roots. In the swamps near Syracuse, fever struck down hundreds of workers. Still, the digging went on, season after season.

The land was not flat, so the builders needed locks to lift and lower the boats. In all, they built 83 of them. At Lockport, five pairs of locks climbed the steep Niagara Escarpment like a giant staircase. Stone aqueducts carried the canal across rivers on bridges of water. The finished channel was about forty feet wide and only four feet deep, just enough for a loaded boat.

In October 1825, after eight years of work, the Erie Canal was finished. Governor Clinton boarded a canal boat called the Seneca Chief in Buffalo and set out for New York City. Along the way, cannons placed within earshot of each other fired one after another, relaying the news across the state in about an hour and a half.

When the flotilla reached New York Harbor, Clinton lifted a keg of water from Lake Erie and poured it into the Atlantic. People called it the Wedding of the Waters. The Great Lakes and the Atlantic Ocean were joined at last.

The results were almost immediate. Before the canal, hauling a ton of goods from Buffalo to New York City could cost about one hundred dollars and take weeks. On the canal, the cost fell to about ten dollars, a drop of roughly ninety percent.

Boats pulled by mules and horses on the towpath carried grain, lumber and salt east, and settlers and manufactured goods west. Towns like Rochester, Syracuse and Buffalo grew from villages into busy cities. Within about a decade, the canal was so crowded that the state began to make it wider and deeper.

Above all, the canal transformed New York City. With a cheap water route to the heart of the continent, it soon became the leading port in the nation, and the canal helped New York earn its nickname, the Empire State.

Clinton's Ditch was no folly after all. It was the ditch that built New York.
